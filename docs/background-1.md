# Music Rights Management — A Layperson's Guide

> How music ownership, royalties, and attribution actually work, and why managing them is so hard.

---

## One Song, Two Copyrights

The single most important thing to understand about music rights is that every recorded song creates **two separate copyrights**:

1. **The composition** (also called the "publishing" side) — the melody, lyrics, and musical arrangement as written. This belongs to the songwriter(s) and their publisher(s).
2. **The master recording** (also called the "master") — the actual audio recording of a performance of that composition. This typically belongs to the artist or, more often, the record label that paid for the recording.

These two copyrights are entirely independent. A single composition can have dozens of master recordings (think of how many artists have recorded "Yesterday" by The Beatles), and each master has its own separate ownership chain.

This dual structure is the root cause of most complexity in music rights management. Every time money flows from a listener to a rights holder, both sides need to be tracked, calculated, and paid — often to completely different people, through completely different channels.

---

## Who Owns What: The Key Players

### Songwriters

The people who write the words and music. A song can have one songwriter or a dozen — modern pop and hip-hop tracks routinely credit five to ten writers. Each songwriter owns a percentage of the composition copyright.

### Publishers

Publishers represent songwriters commercially. They register songs with royalty collection organisations, negotiate licensing deals (e.g. getting a song into a film or advert), and collect royalties on the songwriter's behalf. In exchange, the publisher typically owns or co-owns a share of the composition copyright.

A songwriter who self-publishes keeps 100% of their publishing share. A songwriter signed to a traditional publishing deal may keep as little as 25% of the income their songs generate, with the publisher taking the rest.

### Artists / Performers

The people who actually perform and record the music. An artist who writes their own songs wears two hats — they are both a songwriter (composition side) and a performer (master side). An artist who records someone else's song only has rights on the master side.

### Record Labels

Labels fund the recording process and, in return, usually own the master recording. The label's deal with the artist determines what percentage of master-side revenue the artist receives — typically 15-25% for a new artist on a major label, though independent deals vary widely.

### Producers

Record producers (the people who shape the sound of a recording in the studio) may receive a share on either side or both. A producer who contributes to the songwriting gets a composition credit. Most producers also negotiate "points" — a percentage of master-side revenue, typically 3-5%.

## The Four Types of Royalties

Music generates income through several distinct royalty streams, each with its own collection mechanism:

### 1. Mechanical Royalties

Paid whenever a composition is **reproduced** — originally for physically pressing vinyl or CDs, now primarily for digital downloads and on-demand streams. In the United States, the mechanical royalty rate is set by law (a "statutory rate") and is currently a fraction of a cent per stream. These royalties flow from the streaming service to the songwriter/publisher.

### 2. Performance Royalties

Paid whenever a song is **performed publicly** — on the radio, in a restaurant, at a concert venue, or streamed on a digital service. These are collected by Performing Rights Organisations (PROs) such as ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC in the US, PRS in the UK, or SOCAN in Canada. The PRO collects a blanket licence fee from the venue or broadcaster and distributes it to registered songwriters and publishers.

Performance royalties are split 50/50 between the songwriter and the publisher. This split is an industry convention enforced by the PROs — a publisher cannot contractually take more than their 50% of performance royalties, even if the publishing agreement gives them a larger share of other income.

### 3. Sync Licensing Fees

Paid when a song is **synchronised** with visual media — a film, TV show, advert, video game, or online video. Sync licences are negotiated individually (there is no statutory rate), and both the composition owner and the master owner must grant permission. This means two separate licences are needed: a "sync licence" from the publisher and a "master use licence" from the label. Fees range from a few hundred dollars for a small indie film to millions for a major advertising campaign.

### 4. Master-Side Royalties

Revenue from the exploitation of the master recording specifically — sales, streams, and licensing. When you stream a song on Spotify, the service pays both a composition royalty (to the publisher/songwriter) and a master royalty (to the label/artist). The master side typically receives the larger share of streaming revenue.

## How Splits Work in Practice

### Composition Splits

When multiple songwriters collaborate, they agree on a "split sheet" — a document stating each writer's percentage ownership of the composition. For example:

- Writer A: 50%
- Writer B: 30%
- Writer C: 20%

These percentages determine how every dollar of composition royalties is divided for the life of the copyright (which, in most jurisdictions, lasts 70 years after the last surviving author's death).

**The problem:** Split sheets are often agreed informally, after the fact, or not at all. Disputes over who contributed what are one of the most common sources of litigation in the music industry. A producer who added a melodic hook in the studio may claim a writing credit the other writers dispute. A featured rapper who freestyled their verse may or may not have a writing share, depending on the agreement.

### Publisher Shares

Each songwriter's share is further divided between the writer and their publisher. If Writer A has a "co-publishing" deal (the most common arrangement for successful songwriters), the split might look like:

- Writer A keeps 75% of their share (their "writer's share" of 50% plus half of the "publisher's share")
- Writer A's publisher keeps 25% of Writer A's share

This means the actual money flow for a song earning $1,000 in mechanical royalties with three writers and two publishers can involve six or more separate payments.

### Master Splits

On the recording side, the label typically takes the majority of revenue and pays the artist a royalty rate. But this royalty rate is applied **after** the label recoups its costs. "Recoupment" means the label deducts recording costs, marketing expenses, tour support, and advances before the artist sees any royalty payments. An artist can have a hit record and still owe money to their label.

Producer points are usually paid from the artist's share, further reducing what the artist receives.

## Territory: Rights Change at Every Border

Copyright is territorial. A songwriter might have different publishers in different countries:

- Publisher A handles the US and Canada
- Publisher B handles the UK and Europe
- Publisher C handles Japan and Southeast Asia

Each publisher only collects royalties generated in their territory. When a song is streamed globally, the revenue must be divided by territory and routed to the correct publisher for each region. This is called "sub-publishing."

The same applies to master recordings — a label might license the master to different distributors in different territories, each with their own royalty rates and payment schedules.

---

## Performing Rights Organisations (PROs) and Collecting Societies

PROs and collecting societies are the intermediaries that make the system function. They cannot be bypassed — venues, broadcasters, and streaming services pay blanket licence fees to PROs rather than negotiating individually with millions of rights holders.

Key organisations include:

| Organisation | Territory | Role |
|---|---|---|
| ASCAP | United States | Performance rights (composition) |
| BMI | United States | Performance rights (composition) |
| SESAC | United States | Performance rights (composition) |
| PRS for Music | United Kingdom | Performance rights (composition) |
| SOCAN | Canada | Performance rights (composition) |
| GEMA | Germany | Performance + mechanical rights |
| SoundExchange | United States | Digital performance rights (master) |
| PPL | United Kingdom | Performance rights (master) |

A songwriter must register each song with their PRO, declaring the title, writers, and ownership splits. If any detail is wrong or missing, royalties may go uncollected or be paid to the wrong person.

Different PROs serve different territories, so international royalty collection requires relationships (or "reciprocal agreements") between societies. Money collected in Germany by GEMA for a song written by a US songwriter affiliated with ASCAP must flow from GEMA to ASCAP and then to the songwriter. This chain can take 12 to 24 months.

## Identifiers: The Serial Numbers of Music

The industry uses several standard identifiers to track works and recordings across systems:

- **ISWC** (International Standard Musical Work Code) — identifies a composition. Format: T-000.000.000-0. One composition, one ISWC, regardless of how many recordings exist.
- **ISRC** (International Standard Recording Code) — identifies a specific recording. Format: CC-XXX-YY-NNNNN. Each remix, live version, or re-recording gets its own ISRC.
- **IPI** (Interested Party Information) — identifies a person or organisation involved in music rights (songwriter, publisher). Assigned by CISAC, the international confederation of collecting societies.
- **ISNI** (International Standard Name Identifier) — a broader identifier for creative contributors, used alongside IPI.

When identifiers are missing, duplicated, or mismatched — which happens constantly — royalties cannot be matched to the correct rights holder. Billions of dollars in royalties are estimated to be "unmatched" at any given time across the global music industry.

## The Statement Ingestion Problem

Streaming services (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube, Amazon Music, etc.) send royalty statements to labels and distributors, typically as large CSV or Excel files. These statements report how many times each recording was streamed, in which territory, and how much revenue was generated.

The challenge is that every service uses a different file format, different column names, different territory codes, and different reporting periods. A mid-size independent label might receive 20+ different statement formats every quarter, each of which must be:

1. Parsed and normalised into a common format
2. Matched to the correct recordings in the catalog (using ISRCs)
3. Converted to a common currency (statements arrive in USD, EUR, GBP, JPY, etc.)
4. Reconciled against previous periods to detect anomalies

This process is still done manually in spreadsheets by many smaller labels and publishers — a significant source of errors and delays.

## Common Problems That Rights Management Software Must Solve

### Underpayment and Under-Reporting

Streaming services sometimes under-report streams or miscategorise territory, resulting in lower royalties. Without automated anomaly detection, these discrepancies go unnoticed.

### Unmatched Royalties ("Black Box" Money)

When a PRO or distributor cannot match a royalty payment to a registered rights holder — because of missing identifiers, misspelled names, or unregistered works — the money sits in a "black box." After a holding period (often 3 years), unclaimed royalties are distributed proportionally to all registered members, meaning successful artists effectively subsidise the administrative failures of others.

### Reversion and Option Clauses

Publishing and recording contracts often include time-limited terms. A publishing deal might last 10 years, after which rights "revert" to the songwriter. Option clauses allow a label to extend a recording contract under certain conditions. Tracking these dates across hundreds or thousands of contracts — and ensuring that royalty payments stop or redirect when terms change — requires rigorous calendar management.

### Advances and Recoupment

Labels and publishers routinely pay advances to artists and songwriters against future royalties. Until the advance is fully recouped from earned royalties, the rights holder receives no additional payments. Tracking recoupment balances across multiple contracts, territories, and royalty streams is one of the more complex accounting challenges in the industry.

### Multiple Writers, Multiple Publishers, Multiple Territories

A single song might have:
- 6 songwriters across 3 countries
- 3 different publishers (one per territory group)
- A label that owns the master but has licensed it to different distributors in different regions
- A sync licence for a TV show that only covers North America

Calculating the correct payment to each party for each use type in each territory, in the correct currency, for the correct accounting period, is the central computational problem that rights management software exists to solve.

---

## AI-Generated Music: The Emerging Complication

AI-generated music introduces new attribution questions that existing frameworks were not designed to handle:

- If an AI tool generates a melody, who owns the composition copyright? Current law in most jurisdictions requires human authorship for copyright protection.
- If AI is trained on copyrighted works, do the original rights holders have a claim on AI-generated output?
- PROs (ASCAP, BMI, SOCAN) have begun permitting registration of compositions with AI involvement, provided a human author contributed meaningfully. Systems must now track "AI contribution" as a metadata field.

This is a rapidly evolving legal area with no settled answers, but any modern rights management system must be flexible enough to accommodate whatever frameworks emerge.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Two copyrights per song** — composition and master — each with separate owners, separate royalty streams, and separate collection mechanisms.
2. **Splits are fractional and layered** — a song's revenue passes through writers, publishers, labels, and producers, each taking their contractual share.
3. **Territory matters** — rights are licensed per-country or per-region, and different parties may control the same song in different parts of the world.
4. **Collection is fragmented** — PROs, distributors, and DSPs each handle different royalty types, in different formats, on different schedules.
5. **Identifiers are critical** — ISWC, ISRC, and IPI numbers are the glue that connects royalties to the correct rights holders. When they break, money gets lost.
6. **The math is hard** — combining territorial splits, use-type rates, currency conversion, recoupment, and multi-party ownership into correct payments is genuinely complex accounting.
7. **The industry is still catching up** — much of this work is still done in spreadsheets, and billions of dollars in royalties go uncollected every year because of administrative failures.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Advance** | An upfront payment from a label or publisher to an artist or songwriter, deducted from future royalties before the recipient receives any further income. |
| **Black box money** | Royalties that collecting societies or distributors cannot match to a registered rights holder, often due to missing or incorrect metadata. After a holding period, typically distributed proportionally to all members. |
| **Blanket licence** | A single licence fee paid by a venue, broadcaster, or streaming service to a PRO, granting the right to use any song in that PRO's catalogue rather than negotiating per-song permissions. |
| **Catalogue** | The complete collection of songs or recordings controlled by a particular publisher, label, or rights holder. |
| **CISAC** | Confédération Internationale des Sociétés d'Auteurs et Compositeurs — the international body coordinating collecting societies worldwide. Maintains the IPI database and the CWR registration standard. |
| **Collecting society** | An organisation that collects royalties on behalf of rights holders. PROs are one type; mechanical rights organisations (e.g. the MLC in the US, MCPS in the UK) are another. |
| **Composition** | The underlying musical work — melody, lyrics, and arrangement — as distinct from any particular recording of it. Also called the "publishing side." |
| **Co-publishing deal** | A publishing agreement where the songwriter retains a share of the publisher's portion of income (typically 50%), resulting in the songwriter receiving 75% of total composition income and the publisher 25%. The most common deal structure for established songwriters. |
| **Copyright** | The legal right to control reproduction, distribution, performance, and adaptation of a creative work. In music, two separate copyrights exist: one for the composition and one for the master recording. |
| **CWR (Common Works Registration)** | A standardised file format maintained by CISAC for bulk-registering musical works with PROs worldwide. A publisher creates one CWR file and submits it to multiple societies. |
| **DDEX** | Digital Data Exchange — a standards body that defines XML message formats for communicating release metadata, sales reports, and rights data between labels, distributors, and streaming services. |
| **DSP (Digital Service Provider)** | A streaming or download platform — Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music, Amazon Music, Tidal, etc. |
| **IPI (Interested Party Information)** | A unique numeric identifier assigned to every songwriter, composer, and publisher registered with a CISAC-affiliated collecting society. Used to match royalty payments to the correct individual or entity. |
| **ISNI (International Standard Name Identifier)** | A broader identifier for creative contributors across industries, used alongside IPI in music contexts. |
| **ISRC (International Standard Recording Code)** | A 12-character identifier permanently assigned to a specific sound recording. Each remix, live version, or re-recording receives its own ISRC. The primary key for matching master-side royalties to recordings. |
| **ISWC (International Standard Musical Work Code)** | An 11-character identifier assigned to a musical composition. One composition has exactly one ISWC regardless of how many recordings of it exist. The primary key for matching composition-side royalties. |
| **Master recording (master)** | The actual audio recording of a performance of a composition. Ownership of the master is separate from ownership of the composition. Typically owned by the record label. |
| **Mechanical royalty** | A royalty paid to the composition owner each time a song is reproduced — originally for physical copies (vinyl, CD), now primarily for digital downloads and on-demand streams. |
| **MLC (Mechanical Licensing Collective)** | The US organisation established by the Music Modernization Act (2018) to collect and distribute mechanical royalties from streaming services to songwriters and publishers. |
| **Option clause** | A contract provision giving one party (usually the label or publisher) the right to extend the agreement for an additional period under specified conditions. |
| **Performance royalty** | A royalty paid to the composition owner when a song is performed publicly — on radio, in a venue, on a streaming service, or in a broadcast. Collected by PROs. |
| **Points** | Industry shorthand for percentage points of master-side revenue. A producer with "3 points" receives 3% of the master recording's income, typically paid from the artist's share. |
| **PRO (Performing Rights Organisation)** | An organisation that licenses and collects performance royalties on behalf of songwriters and publishers. Major PROs include ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC (US), PRS (UK), SOCAN (Canada), and GEMA (Germany). |
| **Publisher** | A company that represents songwriters commercially — registering works, negotiating licences, collecting royalties, and exploiting the catalogue. In exchange, the publisher owns or co-owns a share of the composition copyright. |
| **Recoupment** | The process by which a label or publisher deducts advances and costs from an artist's or songwriter's earned royalties before paying them any additional income. Until the advance is "recouped," the rights holder receives nothing beyond the original advance. |
| **Reversion** | The return of rights from a publisher or label to the original creator, typically triggered by the expiration of a contract term or the exercise of a contractual clause. |
| **Split sheet** | A written agreement between co-writers specifying each person's percentage ownership of a composition. The definitive document for determining who gets paid what. |
| **Statutory rate** | A royalty rate set by law rather than negotiated between parties. In the US, the mechanical royalty rate for physical and download sales is a statutory rate set by the Copyright Royalty Board. |
| **Sub-publishing** | An arrangement where a publisher in one territory licenses a publisher in another territory to administer and collect royalties for the same catalogue in that foreign territory. |
| **Sync licence (synchronisation licence)** | Permission to use a composition in combination with visual media (film, TV, advertising, video games). Requires a separate "master use licence" from the recording owner. Fees are negotiated individually. |
| **Territory** | A geographic region (usually a country) for which specific rights are granted or administered. Rights deals are almost always territory-specific. |
| **Writer's share** | The portion of performance royalties paid directly to the songwriter by a PRO. By industry convention, PROs always split performance royalties 50% to the writer and 50% to the publisher, regardless of the publishing contract terms. The writer's share cannot be assigned away. |
